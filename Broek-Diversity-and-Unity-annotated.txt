THESIS
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Written in April 1944, before Japan's defeat, the article argues that
expelling Japan will not restore the colonial status quo of 1940. The demand
for political freedom will be stronger. Economic dependence on the West will
remain, or grow. Immediate fusion of the former colonies into one or two
states ignores the region's diversity. A functional regional bloc, built by
cooperation on concrete problems, can produce unity without erasing that
diversity. Broek's closing word is interdependence, set beside independence.


FIVE ANSWERS, WITH THE PAGES THEY REST ON
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Q1  Japanese occupation.
    Within a few months about 150 million people changed rulers. Economic,
    cultural, and political relations, and mental attitudes, changed in both
    East and West, so a return to the prewar status quo is undesirable and
    impossible. Japan granted a nominal "independence" inside a co-prosperity
    sphere. Western powers answered with political promises. After defeat,
    the desire for freedom will be stronger, while economic dependence on
    the West will be the same or greater. Japan also transferred parts of
    French Indochina, British Malaya, and Burma to Thailand. Those boundaries
    will leave ill feeling. Japan's entry into French Indochina doomed the
    whole structure of Western domination.
    Pages: 175-176; Figure 1 caption; 183; 189; 191; 194.

Q2  Geographical diversity and unity.
    Lowlands are broken up by seas or mountains. Volcanic soils (Sumatra to
    the Banda Sea, and Formosa through the Philippines to northern Celebes
    and Halmahera) contrast with the leached soils of Malaya, Borneo, and
    western New Guinea. People concentrate on wet-rice plains. On the
    mainland, north-south ranges force linear migrations and separate
    political cores. In the archipelago the sea links languages, but it does
    not produce one state. Colonial boundaries later cut the Malay world
    apart. Empty-looking plains in Borneo and New Guinea cannot absorb the
    surplus population of Java and Tongking.
    Pages: 177; Figures 1-4; 182-183; 188.

Q3  Colonial powers and the economic landscape.
    Each power ruled by the ideology of the home country: Americans stressed
    political development, the French cultural assimilation, the British
    economic progress in a laissez-faire form, the Dutch economic progress
    in a paternal form. Trade faced the metropole and turned its back on the
    region. Intra-regional commerce was mainly Singapore transshipment and
    rice from Cochinchina, Thailand, and Burma to plantation areas. French
    Indochina and the Philippines sent one-half to three-fourths of their
    trade to France and the United States. Export of raw materials paid for
    roads, ports, waterworks, and hospitals. Synthetics and new tropical
    production in South America, Africa, and India threaten that model.
    Pages: 176; 182; 187-188; 190-192.

Q4  Hinduism, Islam, and Chinese traditions.
    Hindu expansion in the early centuries CE brought religion, philosophy,
    literature, architecture, and political organization. Angkor and
    Borobudur are the symbols. Living Hinduism survives as a relic on Bali.
    Chinese cultural influence is limited overall, but dominant on the Annam
    coast: Confucianism, Buddhism, ancestor worship, and a mandarin court.
    Islam arrived about the fourteenth century with Muslim merchants from
    northwestern India. By the time the Portuguese arrived it held the
    coasts of the Indies and reached Mindanao. That line explains why Spain
    could Christianize most Filipinos and could not convert the Moros.
    Pages: 183; Figure 6; 186-187.

Q5  A regional bloc: benefits and challenges.
    Challenges: one or two mandated super-states ignore diversity. Filipinos
    awaiting independence would reject a Malayan superstate under
    international supervision. Indonesians would fear the Filipinos, friction
    between Muslims and Christians, and the concentrated Chinese minority in
    British Malaya. Burmese, Thai, and Annamese national consciousness is
    already strong. Japan's territorial gifts to Thailand create ill feeling.
    Six or more national states could become an "Asiatic Balkans."
    Benefits: a joint stand on raw materials; common policy on immigration,
    labor, shipping, and aviation; exchange of information on education,
    hygiene, nutrition, resettlement, credit, agriculture, and industry.
    The form he wants is a secretariat and committees, on the model of the
    Anglo-American Caribbean Commission, not an immediate territorial union.
    Pages: 177; 188-190; 192-195.

p. 175   OPENING
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The first half of the 1940s may be the most decisive period in the history
of Southeast Asia. At the start of the decade, the lands between China,
India, and Australia were under the direct control of Western nations,
except for Thailand, which served as a buffer state. Within a few months,
about 150 million people in the region came under Japanese rule. Driving
the Japanese out will not turn the wheel of fortune full circle and restore
1940. The war has changed economic, cultural, and political relations, and
mental attitudes, in the East and in the West. A return to the prewar
status quo is undesirable and impossible.

    NOTE Q1 | PPT #4, Question 1; also the red line on Handout #4, p. 5,
             "Unattainable Return to Pre-War Status Quo"
    This is the article's summary judgment on the occupation. The facts he
    gives are "a few months" and "150 million inhabitants." The effects are
    four changes: economic, cultural, political, and mental. The conclusion
    is that the prewar status quo is both undesirable and impossible.
    Handout #4, p. 4, repeats the same 150-million figure. Broek does not
    here describe Japanese military administration, labor conscription, or
    nationalist organizations. The impact is stated as a structural break.

The colonial question is therefore more important than ever. It is
understandable that debate centers on political independence. That subject
absorbs so much attention that economic and social questions of equal
weight are barely discussed. The anticolonial tradition in American history
produces a strong emotional protest against dependent territories. That
attitude can push reform forward, but only if one sees the relation as a
deep-rooted cultural and economic complex, not as an accidental and immoral
political tie. Colonial rule is one form of acculturation, and therefore a
passing phase. Western Europe was once a dependency of Rome. The more
advanced Mediterranean civilization exploited it and also enriched it.
Western Europe later threw off Roman rule and in time surpassed the old
empire.

    NOTE Q3 / colonialism definition | PPT #4, "What is Colonialism?"
    The handout defines colonialism as domination by conquest, economic
    control, and the imposition of language and culture. Broek accepts the
    fact of domination, then moves the definition. For him the colonial
    relation is a form of acculturation, a transitory cultural and economic
    complex, not only a moral scandal. The Roman analogy is his evidence
    that a dependency can later surpass its metropole. The five national
    "templates" that match the handout's phrase "each reflected their own
    historical experience" appear later, on p. 188.

p. 176   "IMPERIAL DEVOLUTION"
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Southeast Asia is in a stage Broek calls imperial devolution. Attention
fixed only on the political side of that process hides the problem as a
whole. Thailand, and some republics of tropical America, show that a higher
standard of living and more democratic procedure do not follow automatically
from political sovereignty. The Philippine commonwealth is moving quickly
toward political independence and remains economically dependent on the
United States. Prosperity elsewhere in Southeast Asia did not rest quite so
heavily on access to a single market. Taken together, however, these lands
stood toward the Western countries much as the Philippines stood toward the
United States.

    NOTE Q3 | colonial economic landscape, first example
    The Philippines are his proof that political independence is not
    economic autonomy. The trade figures that support this sentence are on
    p. 187: one-half to three-fourths of Philippine trade was with the
    United States, and the same range of French Indochinese trade was with
    France.

Japan now grants "independence" generously across Southeast Asia and at the
same time insists on a co-prosperity sphere. Japan will rule. Local peoples
will remain in a subordinate role, the hewers of wood and drawers of water.
Western nations, answering Japanese propaganda, make political promises of
their own. A strange result follows. After Japan's defeat, the ambition of
local peoples for political freedom will be stronger than before, while
their economic dependence on the West will be the same or greater. The gap
between political ideals and economic realities will be the most troubling
problem of postwar tropical Asia.

    NOTE Q1 | PPT #4, pp. 4-5: the occupation "exposed weaknesses of Western
             dominance" and "intensified nationalist movements"
    This paragraph is the passage the handout is compressing. What the
    article actually says is narrower than the handout. Japan raised
    political expectations with the words "independence" and "co-prosperity
    sphere," while keeping local peoples in a subordinate economic role.
    Western governments answered with promises. Therefore, after Japan's
    defeat, the ambition for political freedom will be stronger, and
    economic dependence on the West will not fall. Broek does not name
    nationalist parties, youth leagues, or armed resistance. If the exam
    sentence is "the occupation intensified nationalist movements," mark it
    as a reading of this paragraph, not as a fact he narrates.

An approach that looks only at political doctrines will end in
disillusion. Peoples in the liberated countries will find that independence
has not reduced the need for markets, capital, and trained personnel, and
has not solved the problem of security. Americans will again be dismayed
that evils which were supposed to disappear with this war for freedom are
still there.

It is often assumed that independent sovereignty for every people is the
final aim of a just world order. The Soviet Union, with its many peoples,
shows a different solution that appears to work: equal partnership. One
cannot rule out in advance the possibility that some peoples of Southeast
Asia would prefer a similar interdependence. Whether the future is
partnership or independence, a period of preparation is required before
that adult status is reached. The road and the time differ from people to
people. They depend as much on social and economic structure as on
political cohesion.

    NOTE Q5 | why he refuses immediate union
    The "period of preparation" is his reason for rejecting both instant
    independence and instant fusion. The Soviet example is a 1944 analogy.
    It opens the possibility that some peoples may prefer partnership over
    sovereignty. It is not a discussion of the Soviet Union itself. The
    same thought closes the article on p. 195, where "interdependence" is
    set beside "independence."

p. 177   REGIONAL DIVERSITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Speeches that demand freedom now and everywhere forget these points. Modern
communications have made the earth one. They have not made it uniform. The
new discovery that the earth is "global" does not change the fact that
peoples belong first to a local culture pattern, each with its own
relations of person to person and person to nature, its own traditions,
institutions, and ways of making a living. Words such as democracy,
self-determination, and independence take their meaning from local
experience, not from global charters. Ready-made universal schemes are
likely to fit badly. The task is to analyze the specific regional problems
and plan from that analysis.

    NOTE Q2 | PPT #4, p. 5: "Natural barriers and colonial boundaries
             reinforced political fragmentation"
    This paragraph supplies the premise. Peoples belong first to a local
    culture pattern. Universal schemes of independence misfit. The natural
    barriers are specified in the next paragraphs and on p. 183. The
    colonial boundaries are specified on pp. 188-189. The handout's one
    sentence joins those two later passages. Use both, not this paragraph
    alone.

Over the past year or so, plans have been put forward to fuse the former
dependencies into one or two political units, administered or supervised by
an international authority or a mandatory power until they are ready for
full self-rule. Whatever other difficulties those plans have, they do not
take the diversity of Southeast Asia seriously enough.

    NOTE Q5 | the challenge side of a regional bloc
    This is the plan he rejects: immediate fusion into one or two mandated
    units. He cites Fortune (August 1942, Pacific supplement) and Ely
    Culbertson, Total Peace (1943), pp. 323-334. The plan he accepts comes
    only on p. 193: a functional bloc, not a superstate. Handout #4 places
    ASEAN beside this question. ASEAN dates from 1967 and is not in the
    article. The honest link is that both are regional and non-fusion at
    the start. Broek's body still includes the colonial powers as
    accountable rulers. ASEAN does not.

The natural environment varies greatly. The dominant fact is that the
lowlands are broken up by sea or by mountains. Great plains once lay where
the Sunda and Sahul shelves are now drowned. Beyond the amphibious coastal
swamps, today's plains are relatively small. Inside the lowlands, soil
fertility differs sharply. The best soils come from volcanic deposits. They
lie along the inner side of the great southern island arc, from Sumatra
east to the fishhook around the Banda Sea, and along the northern arc, part
of the East Asiatic island festoons, from Formosa through the Philippines
south to northern Celebes and Halmahera (Figure 1).

    NOTE Q2 | first geographical layer
    Fragmented lowlands, drowned shelves, and a volcanic-soil arc. This is
    the physical base of the later claim that the region cannot be one
    economy or one state. British Malaya, Borneo, and western New Guinea
    are the contrast: non-volcanic, and heavily leached. That sentence
    begins at the bottom of p. 177 and continues on p. 182.

British Malaya, Borneo, and western New Guinea, by contrast, are
non-volcanic, and heavy equatorial rain has leached their soils thoroughly.
The sentence continues on p. 182.

FIGURE 2   RAINFALL (map plate)

Areas marked 1, including Burma, most of Thailand, interior French
Indochina, the northwestern Philippines, and the middle Lesser Sunda
Islands, have a pronounced dry monsoon. Where those areas face the
rain-bearing winds, as on the Burma coast and in the western Philippines,
the wet monsoon is so heavy that the annual total exceeds 80 inches and can
exceed the rainfall of equatorial areas. Akyab, on the Arakan coast,
reaches 207 inches. Areas marked 2 have a less marked dry season. Java is
under the Asian northwest monsoon from October to May. From April to
November the southeast monsoon prevails. It brings rain to the south coast
but is generally drier than the northwest monsoon. Western Java and the
whole south coast therefore have high annual rainfall and no dry season,
while the northeast has months that are practically dry.

p. 182   SOILS, SETTLEMENT, MIGRATION
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Continued from p. 177. Leaching limits the range of suitable crops, and
yields are lower than on volcanic land. Burma, Thailand, and French
Indochina have no active volcanoes. Their alluvial soils are on the whole
less rich than Java's, but they are also less leached than the soils of
the equatorial zone.

Settlement is largely a search for wet-rice fields, and it reflects those
differences. Population is highly concentrated on the alluvial plains. New
Guinea, Borneo, Sumatra, and Malaya are exceptions, because swamp or
leached soil prevails there (Figure 3). Outside forces have sharpened the
contrasts rather than smoothed them. Chinese dike building and intensive
farming raised the carrying capacity of the Red River delta and the Annam
coast. Java and Luzon, the most favored islands of the Malay world, became
centers of colonial development, and their populations have grown tenfold
since the early nineteenth century. In these three areas population
pressure has reached a danger point. In lower Tongking and on Java the
average density is about 1,000 persons to the square mile. In central
Luzon it is 600, but poor conditions of land tenure make the pressure
greater than the density figure alone suggests.

    NOTE PPT3 | "Little China" / Sinicization; also NOTE Q3 and NOTE Q4
    "The Chinese arts of dike building and intensive farming" is the only
    concrete case in which Chinese technique raises carrying capacity.
    The places are the Red River delta and the Annam coast, the same region
    as the handout's northern Vietnam. Broek does not use "Little China,"
    and he does not give the dates 111 BCE-939 CE, the Dong Son culture,
    or the sequence of Vietnamese dynasties on Handout #3, p. 3.
    The tenfold population increase on Java and Luzon is attributed to
    colonial development, so the same paragraph also serves Question 3.

Censuses of recent decades show that, even after ample allowance for errors
of enumeration, the average annual rate of growth in some thinly settled
areas has been much higher than in the old cores (Figure 4). This does not
mean that population is spreading evenly over the region. In a nearly empty
district a small absolute increase produces a high percentage. What it does
mean is that modern civilization, in the form of roads, hygiene, or the
development of resources, lifts some potentially favorable areas to a
higher level of opportunity, for local people or for immigrants. The high
rates in southern Sumatra and especially in Mindanao reflect the arrival of
Javanese and Filipinos. Rates in western Malaya and around Medan in eastern
Sumatra are also strongly affected by immigration, but most of those
immigrants were birds of passage. They came in large numbers during the
boom in rubber estates and tin mines, and they left in the depression of
the early 1930s. Elsewhere, high rates, if they are not bad census
estimates, come mainly from increase of the local population.

    NOTE Q3 | export crops redraw the population map
    Rubber estates and tin mines pull temporary migrants into western
    Malaya and the Medan area. Their departure in the early 1930s is the
    first sign of the export fragility he develops on pp. 191-192.
    Javanese movement to southern Sumatra and Filipino movement to Mindanao
    reappear on p. 192 as the resettlement policy he wants enlarged.

p. 183   CARRYING CAPACITY, LANGUAGE, "FARTHER INDIA"
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The sparse settlement of the plains of Borneo and New Guinea, for example,
is not an accident. Their carrying capacity is inherently lower than that
of the present crowded areas. Respect for the rights of the local
populations requires that large tracts of arable land be reserved for their
future needs. The land available for resettling surplus people from the
crowded districts is therefore much smaller than it first appears. That
fact undercuts Japanese claims to living space in these southern regions,
and it warns against postwar schemes to colonize the same lands with
European refugees.

    NOTE Q1 | geography used against a Japanese claim
    This is not a battle narrative. It uses carrying capacity to reject
    Japan's claim to living space in the southern regions. Pair it with
    p. 175 if the question asks for "impacts," and keep the two impacts
    distinct: p. 175 is the political and mental break; this page is the
    rejection of the occupation's demographic justification.

Racial distribution also reflects the natural environment. Rugged,
heavily forested uplands are barriers between the lowlands. This is
especially true on the mainland, where north-south ranges have forced
migrations into linear paths. In the archipelago the sea has been a link
rather than a barrier, so coastal and interior peoples form a pattern
closer to concentric circles. Successive eastward migrations have all left
traces, from the oldest recognizable element, the Negrito, in remote jungle
retreats, to the latest and dominant group, the Mongoloid peoples. For the
postwar world, language groups matter more, because they are an important
factor in the nations that exist or are emerging in Southeast Asia
(Figure 5).

    NOTE Q2 | two geographies, two obstacles to unity
    Mainland: mountains are barriers, so migrations are linear, and the
    river valleys become separate political cores. Archipelago: the sea is
    a link, so the language map is simpler. Political unity does not follow
    in either case. On the mainland the cores are already different
    peoples. In the islands a shared language base is later cut by colonial
    rule (p. 188). Question 2 is answered only when this page is joined to
    p. 188.

The separated valleys of the Irrawaddy, the Menam, the Mekong, and the Red
River, and the narrow coastal plain of Annam, have been the migration
routes, cultural cores, and political key areas of the Burmese, the Thai,
the Cambodians, and the Annamese. Repeated migrations and cultural impacts
have produced a highly complex situation. Earlier settlers were absorbed by
later groups or pushed into the uplands. Mon-Khmer peoples now appear as
fragments across Farther India. Their once solid hold on the region was
torn apart by Burmese, Thai, and Annamese invasions. The mainland branch of
the Malayo-Polynesian languages in French Indochina, the Cham and related
peoples, survives as a much reduced group in the mountain refuge of the
central Annamese range. The most recent immigrants appear to be the
Miao-Yao tribes, who have moved into the northern part of the area over
recent centuries, probably under pressure from the Chinese proper.

    NOTE PPT3 | "Farther India"; also NOTE Q4, the geographical base
    This is the sentence in which Broek uses "Farther India." Handout #3
    defines Farther India as Indianization, about 100-1000 CE. Broek uses
    the name for the mainland's ethnic fragmentation: Mon-Khmer peoples
    reduced to fragments by Burmese, Thai, and Annamese invasions. The
    Indian cultural layer is not described until p. 186. The two uses are
    related. They are not the same statement. The Miao-Yao movement under
    Chinese pressure is a migration fact, not a statement about Confucianism.

Against the extreme complexity of the mainland, the basic language pattern
of the island world is fairly simple. That again shows the unifying work of
the sea. Continued on p. 186.

p. 186   INDIAN, CHINESE, AND ISLAMIC LAYERS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Continued from p. 183. From the Malay Peninsula northward, including part
of Formosa, and eastward through the Moluccas, except for northern
Halmahera, which is Papuan in speech, the languages belong to one stock:
the Malayan division of the Malayo-Polynesian family. Inside that division
there are still many languages. The Philippines have at least 60 languages
and dialects. The Netherlands Indies have about 25 languages and some 250
dialects.

The social patchwork visible in Southeast Asia today comes only partly from
the wanderings of peoples now counted as indigenous. The differences among
them would be rather small had outside forces not struck them: first the
more advanced civilizations of the Orient, later the Western world.

Before Westerners found the way to tropical Asia, the region had for
centuries been a colonial area for both Hindus and Chinese. That meant, as
it still means, exploitation by profit-seeking merchants and by conquerors
who wanted tribute, and also cultural enrichment, either by active
proselytizing or by imitation and adaptation. Hindu expansion in the early
centuries of the common era, in particular, brought higher forms of
religion, philosophy, literature, and architecture, and higher forms of
political and social organization, into tribal societies that had been
primitive and pagan. The temples and palaces of present-day Burma,
Thailand, and Bali, and classic monuments such as Angkor in Cambodia and
Borobudur in Java, are symbols of that impulse from India. The cultural

REGIONAL PROBLEMS

Given these contrasts and divergent interests, one may well ask whether
there is any point in treating Southeast Asia as a regional unit. The
answer is that, in spite of all the differences just noted, certain broader
considerations of far-reaching significance still make the concept valid.

The first is the colonial character of the region. In recent years there
has been growing acceptance of the view that colonial powers are trustees
for the areas they rule and also owe the rest of the world a moral duty to
account for their stewardship. International administration over the whole
region is neither practical nor desirable. There is much to be said,
however, for a regional supervisory organ that would embody this
third-party interest, at least if the same principle is applied to all
colonial areas in the world. On that line of thought, after Japan is
expelled the colonial powers would resume charge of their respective
dependencies, but they would be accountable to an international authority.

The function of that international body should not be limited to
supervising progress toward self-government. Political rights mean little
unless they rest on a sound social and economic foundation. If outside
powers, in the spirit of the Atlantic Charter, want a part in the political

responsibility for the material welfare of those lands. It would be strange
if public opinion in the United States could demand full democracy in
Malaya and at the same time disclaim any concern for economic conditions
there. That raises the economic future of Southeast Asia to first
importance.

p. 190   THE RAW-MATERIAL EXPORT ECONOMY

In the past, exports of commodities taken from field, forest, and mine

naturally poor in capital. Investment by Westerners, and to a smaller

Since about the middle of the nineteenth century, Southeast Asia has gone
through an extraordinary economic development. While the economy of Latin
America stagnated because slavery was abolished and governments were
unstable, colonial Southeast Asia, brought closer to Europe by the Suez
Canal, became the great tropical supply center for Western industry. That
paramount position is now seriously threatened from two directions.
Preparation for war, and the war itself, led industrial countries to
develop synthetic substitutes and led the tropical parts of India, Africa,
and especially South America to introduce or expand natural production. Not
all of these ventures will succeed. If even a part of them survive the war,
Southeast Asia faces considerable competition. The competition will not be
only a matter of price and quality, on which tropical Asia might on the
whole be stronger. It will also involve political and national
considerations. The lesson that it is dangerous to rely on distant
countries for strategic raw materials may lead Europe and America to
maintain home production of synthetics at whatever cost. The large capital
sunk in the new industries will support that policy. Synthetic rubber in

States to prefer the new or revived products of that region. Wider outlets
elsewhere can make up part of the loss. If China is helped to its feet, and
if its transport and industry develop so that consuming power rises, China
may become a substantial customer for rubber, fibers, industrial fats and
oils, and foodstuffs. Even then it is hard to see how Southeast Asia can
regain its former export volume. The shock to the social and economic

their responsibility is greater than an expression of sympathy with
political ideals.

pp. 191-192   NEW OPPORTUNITIES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The future requires a new approach. It is too early to draw up any specific
long-range plan, and the problems differ from country to country. A few
brief suggestions can show the possible directions.

If a constructive policy of readjustment meets it, the decline of exports
may to some extent be a blessing in disguise. A shift of emphasis from a
few export commodities to a more diversified agriculture would increase
economic resistance in years of depression. The disastrous early 1930s
taught a hard lesson, and some useful changes did occur, but they were
small beside what the postwar period will require. British Malaya in
particular needs to grow more of its own food. In a normal year it imported
about two-thirds of the rice it consumed.

Another item in a long-range reconstruction is resettlement, either to
relieve population pressure in overcrowded areas or to give a new
livelihood to workers in depressed export industries. In the preceding
decade there were energetic efforts to move people from Java to Sumatra and
from Luzon to Mindanao. The first results were promising and still too
small to give any material relief. The work will have to be resumed on a
much larger scale. The number of colonists leaving Java rose from about
20,000 in 1937 to about 53,000 in 1940. In the Philippines, the National
Land Settlement Administration reported that 11,500 persons were settled in
Mindanao in 1939 and 1940.

The development of manufacturing is another road to improvement, and a more
stimulating one than the spread of subsistence agriculture under
resettlement schemes. It will also be a necessity if shrunken export
markets do not provide enough money to buy manufactured goods abroad. The
results obtained on Java in the 1930s, under a social policy that
encouraged small factories making daily necessities, are guideposts. Besides
Java, central Luzon, lower Tongking, and possibly the districts around
Rangoon, Bangkok, and Singapore may become centers of consumer-goods
industries, selling at an Oriental price level. That activity in turn
induces more basic industries, as Java's experience has already shown.
Such industrial centers will also supply the outlying agrarian regions, and
closer trade relations will result. This was already visible in the
Netherlands Indies. Under the stimulus of expanding factory production, the
value of Java's exports of manufactured goods to the other islands rose
from 34 million guilders in 1934 to 70 million in 1940.

    NOTE Q5 | the benefit inside the region, before the institutions
    These three proposals are the economic program a bloc would coordinate:
    more diverse food agriculture (Malaya imported about two-thirds of its

Whether Southeast Asia will develop a heavy industry is still an academic
question. The possibility cannot be dismissed merely because the region has
little or no metallurgical coal and relatively small iron deposits. Those
twin elements of the early Industrial Revolution are becoming less
important as technology advances. Heavy rainfall and strong relief give
Southeast Asia considerable potential water power. Metal alloys and
plastics are bound to play a much larger role than in the past. The region
has many metals, including tin, aluminum, chromium, manganese, and
tungsten, and enormous forest reserves that may one day supply the raw
material for wood plastics.

Industrialization, and the changes in social and economic organization that
come with it, will tend to lower the birth rate, as has happened in other
countries. Only by that process can one hope to break the vicious circle in
which a rise in living standards is cancelled by the survival of more
people.

The growth of manufacturing will again depend largely on Western nations
for capital, equipment, and technical assistance. This may look like an
invitation to the present industrial countries to cut their own throats.
Experience has shown, however, that the flow of trade among industrial
countries is greater than the flow between industrial countries and
producers of raw materials.

p. 194   LOCATIONAL UNITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------

This idea of a community of interests, seen from inside or from outside,
also appears in Southeast Asia's character as a transit region. That has
long been recognized for the Malay Archipelago and the adjacent peninsula.
These lands astride the equator are at once a barrier guarding the gateway
between the Pacific and Indian Oceans and a line of stepping stones between
Asia and Australia. Together with the other two bottlenecks of world
shipping, the Caribbean with the Panama Canal and the Mediterranean with
the Suez Canal, the Malay world forms the trinity of strategic thoroughfares
on which world sea power rests. French Indochina and the Philippines flank
the South China Sea and guard the approaches to the Strait of Malacca and
the Sunda Strait. Thailand borders both the Pacific and the Indian Ocean
and controls the Isthmus of Kra, the potential site of a canal linking the
two oceans. In recent years the rise of Burma as a transit zone between
China and the Indian Ocean has reinforced this strategic position. Air
transport will certainly play a large role after the war. It will not
replace transport by land and sea. The future system of skyways is likely
even to accentuate Southeast Asia's place as a crossroads. The great-circle
route from the Pacific coast of America via Japan to eastern China, the
Philippines, and Singapore meets here the transpacific island-hopping line
via Hawaii, and also the route from Australia to India and onward to West
Asia and Europe.

    NOTE PPT3 | "The Malay Archipelago"
    This is where Broek uses the name. He uses it for a strategic
    thoroughfare between the Pacific and the Indian Ocean, and as stepping
    stones between Asia and Australia. He ranks it with the Caribbean and

    title page of Alfred Russel Wallace's The Malay Archipelago (1869) and
    with Mouhot's travel book. Wallace is not in the article.

These functions give Southeast Asia a vital place in the world's
circulatory system and make its security a matter of international concern.
In this respect no part of the region can be separated from the whole. When
Japan penetrated French Indochina, the entire structure of Western
domination in Southeast Asia was doomed. In the same way, once Japan is
expelled from Burma, from Sumatra, or from the Philippine Islands, its
whole newly won empire will start to crumble.

p. 195   SIX STATES, AN "ASIATIC BALKANS," INTERDEPENDENCE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

This strategic interdependence bears even more directly on the fate of the
peoples of Southeast Asia as they approach self-rule. Historical
development points clearly to the eventual emergence of at least six
national states in the region. They will be flanked by Australia, India,
and China, and to the north by Japan, which despite defeat may remain a
potentially formidable nation. If one leaves open for the moment the
question of Japan's future power, and sets Australia aside because in
population it is a small nation and remote from most of the Southeast Asian
countries, the contrast between Southeast Asia and its immediate neighbors
becomes even sharper.

The thought then arises that the fragmentation of Southeast Asia into six
or more separate states may create an Asiatic Balkans, destined to become a
pawn in an eventual struggle for political or economic supremacy among its
great neighbors. Speculation of that kind can hardly be a compelling
argument for a regional bloc. There are, however, other and urgent problems
that require a regional solution. Cooperation on the concrete questions of
colonial emancipation, security, and welfare may gradually establish a
sense of unity that rises above local divergences. Through that organic
evolution toward interdependence, Southeast Asia can become strong enough
to ward off outside pressure and to gain genuine freedom.

    NOTE Q5 | benefits he is willing to claim, and the one he discounts
    He discounts the "Asiatic Balkans" fear as a compelling argument by
    itself. The compelling arguments are the concrete problems already
    listed: colonial emancipation, security, and welfare. Cooperation on

    bloc will appear fully formed in 1945.
    The "at least six national states" are not named in a list. From the
    political units he has discussed, they are Burma, Thailand, Annam
    (Vietnam), British Malaya, the Netherlands Indies, and the Philippines.
    Cambodia and Laos are treated as weaker cores, not as two more of the six.

The concept of the national sovereign state is essentially a product of
European culture. No one will deny the stimulating quality of a vigorous
national life. No one can fail to see the chaos caused by nationalistic
anarchy. While the Western world searches for a way out of that impasse,
tropical Asia is struggling toward national self-determination.

Must these peoples travel the same road Europe traveled, or can they
subordinate patriotism to collective regional interests? If the Western
nations recognize their true responsibility, it seems not yet too late to
guide Southeast Asia toward a future in which interdependence will be as
challenging a word as independence is today.

   The article's bloc is a 1944 proposal for functional consultation, with
   colonial powers still in place and accountable. ASEAN is a later
   association of independent states. Compare their functions. Do not write
   that Broek proposed ASEAN.

Handout #4, p. 6, sets ASEAN (8 August 1967) beside the European Union
(1 November 1993), the African Union (9 July 2002), and the Union of South
American Nations. The handout dates UNASUR to 23 May 2019. Check that date
before repeating it. The treaty is more often dated 2008, with entry into
force in 2011. Broek's bloc is expressly not a territorial or supranational
union of the European kind.

p. 1  Seven regional names: Farther India, Little China, Nusantara, Malay

p. 2  Indianization, ca. 100-1000 CE. Corresponds in substance to p. 186,
      which says "the early centuries of our era" and does not use the term.
      Sinicization of northern Vietnam, 111 BCE-939 CE. The article's
      Chinese material is p. 182 (dikes and farming) and p. 186 (Annam coast).

p. 3  The Vietnam-China chronology is from Keith Taylor, The Birth of
      Vietnam (1983), as the slide says. It is not in Broek.
      Nusantara, Kertanagara (1268-1292), Melayu Raya, Parameswara, and
      Melaka's adoption of Islam in the early 1400s are not in Broek.
      The article's Malay discussion is the political refusal of a Malayan
      superstate on p. 188.

p. 4  EIC and VOC, and the list of colonies (Dutch East Indies, Spanish

      Spice blanks: cloves; nutmeg and mace. Not in the article.

p. 7  Founding members shown on the map: Indonesia, Malaysia, the

      agreed in principle as the 11th member. That sentence is the slide's,
